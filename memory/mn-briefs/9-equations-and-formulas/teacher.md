## What this topic must teach

A student finishes this topic holding these ideas. Everything else in the
brief serves them.

1. **Whatever you do to one side, do to the other.** That is the only rule; every technique is an application of it. A student who holds the balance idea can solve equations they have never seen a template for.

2. **Rearranging a formula is solving an equation whose letters happen to have names.** Solving for h in A = ½bh is the same move as solving for x. Students treat literal equations as a new topic; they are not.

3. **Some equations have no solution, and some have every solution.** When the variable disappears, the sentence that remains is either true or false, and that answers the question. Meet both cases deliberately rather than as an accident.

**The error to design against:** Distributing a negative sign across a bracket and dropping it on the later terms — −(x − 3) becoming −x − 3.

If your Mongolian version lands those and a student can do the practice
set, the topic is right — however you got there.

Each lesson, and what the student must end up able to do:

### 1. `variables-on-both-sides`

**Able to:** Solve equations with variables on both sides by collecting like terms across the equals sign.

**The idea that carries it:** Herd the variables to one side (subtract the smaller x-term), the numbers to the other — then it's a two-step equation.

**Where students break** — the English warns about these, and they are
worth keeping however you phrase them:
- Moving a term without changing its sign.
- Herding onto the side that creates negatives.

**3 worked examples.** Yours to choose — different numbers, a
different context, a Mongolian setting instead of an American one. What
each must *do* is fixed:
- `eqf1-we1` — Solve $3x - 4 = 2x + 6$.
- `eqf1-we2` — Plan A costs $20 + 5g$ dollars; Plan B costs $8g$. For what data amount do they cost the same?
- `eqf1-we3` — Solve $7 - 2x = 4x - 11$.

**2 try-it problems**, same freedom and same condition.

### 2. `special-cases`

**Able to:** Recognize identities (all real numbers) and contradictions (no solution) when the variables cancel.

**The idea that carries it:** Variables cancel + true statement → all reals; variables cancel + false statement → no solution.

**Where students break** — the English warns about these, and they are
worth keeping however you phrase them:
- Writing x = 0 when everything cancels.
- Grinding on after reaching a false statement.

**3 worked examples.** Yours to choose — different numbers, a
different context, a Mongolian setting instead of an American one. What
each must *do* is fixed:
- `eqf2-we1` — Solve $2(x + 3) = 2x + 6$.
- `eqf2-we2` — Solve $2(x + 3) = 2x + 1$.
- `eqf2-we3` — Solve $5x - 2(x + 4) = 3x - 8$.

**2 try-it problems**, same freedom and same condition.

### 3. `multi-step-pipelines`

**Able to:** Solve equations combining parentheses, fractions, and variables on both sides with a reliable order of moves.

**The idea that carries it:** Distribute → clear fractions → herd variables → two-step → check. Five small moves beat one heroic leap.

**Where students break** — the English warns about these, and they are
worth keeping however you phrase them:
- Multiplying only the fraction terms when clearing.
- Distributing a negative to the first term only.

**3 worked examples.** Yours to choose — different numbers, a
different context, a Mongolian setting instead of an American one. What
each must *do* is fixed:
- `eqf3-we1` — Solve $\frac{2(x - 3)}{5} + 1 = \frac{x}{2}$.
- `eqf3-we2` — Solve $3(2x - 1) - 4(x + 2) = 7$.
- `eqf3-we3` — Solve $\frac{x + 4}{3} = \frac{2x - 1}{5}$.

**2 try-it problems**, same freedom and same condition.

### 4. `literal-equations`

**Able to:** Rearrange formulas and literal equations to isolate any chosen variable.

**The idea that carries it:** Solve for a letter with the same undo moves — the 'answer' is an expression built from the other letters.

**Where students break** — the English warns about these, and they are
worth keeping however you phrase them:
- Dividing only one term by the coefficient.
- Treating the target letter as special mid-solve.

**3 worked examples.** Yours to choose — different numbers, a
different context, a Mongolian setting instead of an American one. What
each must *do* is fixed:
- `eqf4-we1` — Solve $A = \frac{1}{2}bh$ for $h$, then find $h$ when $A = 24$ and $b = 6$.
- `eqf4-we2` — Solve $C = \frac{5}{9}(F - 32)$ for $F$, and convert $C = 25$.
- `eqf4-we3` — Solve $P = 2\ell + 2w$ for $w$.

**2 try-it problems**, same freedom and same condition.

### 5. `modeling-with-equations`

**Able to:** Model consecutive-integer, geometry, and mixed-quantity problems with multi-step equations.

**The idea that carries it:** One variable, everything expressed from it, one equation from the story's built-in rule — then translate back.

**Where students break** — the English warns about these, and they are
worth keeping however you phrase them:
- Using two variables when one suffices.
- Answering with n when the question asked for something else.

**3 worked examples.** Yours to choose — different numbers, a
different context, a Mongolian setting instead of an American one. What
each must *do* is fixed:
- `eqf5-we1` — Three consecutive integers sum to $84$. Find them.
- `eqf5-we2` — A rectangle's length is $3$ more than twice its width; the perimeter is $36$. Find the dimensions.
- `eqf5-we3` — $28$ people paid \$188: adults \$9, kids \$5. How many adults?

**2 try-it problems**, same freedom and same condition.

### 6. `rate-time-and-mixture`

**Able to:** Solve motion (d = rt) and mixture problems by organizing rate × time or amount × concentration in tables.

**The idea that carries it:** Rate × time = amount, one row per actor; the story says whether the amounts add, match, or average.

**Where students break** — the English warns about these, and they are
worth keeping however you phrase them:
- Adding the rates when travelers move the same direction.
- Averaging concentrations without volumes.

**3 worked examples.** Yours to choose — different numbers, a
different context, a Mongolian setting instead of an American one. What
each must *do* is fixed:
- `eqf6-we1` — Trains $300$ km apart travel toward each other at $70$ and $80$ km/h. When do they meet?
- `eqf6-we2` — A cyclist leaves at $12$ km/h. Two hours later, a car follows at $60$ km/h. How long until the car catches up?
- `eqf6-we3` — How many liters of $20\%$ juice must mix with $50\%$ juice to make $10$ L of $30\%$ juice?

**2 try-it problems**, same freedom and same condition.

## What you owe for every example you change

A worked example or try-it problem with different numbers needs a new
**check** — a line of maths that says what must be true, so we can verify it
mechanically without a second person re-solving it.

**You do not write these.** Write the example and its answer in plain
Mongolian, and Build turns it into the assertion. If an example's answer
cannot be stated exactly, the example is not ready.

**Practice and test-yourself problems are different: do not change their
numbers.** Those are graded against a stored answer key and every student's
history is keyed to them. Rewrite the *wording* freely; leave the maths.

### Careful — English uses one word where Mongolian uses two

These are the ones that go wrong silently. The word above is the sense
this topic most likely means; check it against what you are actually
writing, and say so if the topic needs the other one.

**amount** — proposed **хэмжээ**

> POLYSEMOUS, and the shipped mirrors genuinely disagree, which is why this is medium. хэмжээ is the dominant choice for "amount" as a quantity of something (three of the four lines given with this term, plus the decimal-division unit throughout) and is what this entry proposes. But when "amounts" means measurable numerical quantities in the data-type sense, shipped switches to хэмжигдэхүүн: "**Numerical** data: amounts you can add and average" → «**Тоон** өгөгдөл: нэмж, дундажилж болох хэмжигдэхүүн», and "Category labels are not amounts" → «Ангиллын шошго бол хэмжигдэхүүн биш». That is the same word the ministry uses as the title of its measurement strand (MoE 10.12 «Хэмжигдэхүүн»). Suggested split: хэмжээ for a concrete amount in a problem, хэмжигдэхүүн for "quantity" as a category of data. Note хэмжээ is also this batch's "size".

**translate** — proposed **хөрвүүлэх**

> Words→symbols sense only; one shipped string instead says «болго». The geometry verb is «параллель зөөх» (MoE 10.11) and the two must not merge — same English word, two Mongolian verbs.

## How it should sound

**«та», formal.** A student on this product is a customer, and «чи» is not
suitable for one. Formal is not the same as stiff — the English has jokes and
energy, and that should survive. Do not flatten it into textbook prose.

Quotes take «...». Proper names take their standard Mongolian forms. Product
names and symbols stay Latin.

> Note if you have seen earlier material: grades 6 and 7 are written in «чи»,
> which is now the wrong register. Do not copy their tone.

## Writing maths

Only the rules that change what you type:

1. **Do not put Mongolian words inside a formula.** Write the formula in
   symbols and the words outside it. (If a word truly must sit inside one,
   mark it and Build will wrap it correctly.)
2. **Letters stay Latin** — `x`, `h`, point names, function names — even in
   Mongolian prose. Gloss one on first use if it helps.
3. **Bold is `**like this**`.** Single asterisks do not work and will appear
   on screen as asterisks.
4. **Units:** см, кг, мл read fine in a sentence.
