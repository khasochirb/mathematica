Each lesson, and what the student must end up able to do:

### 1. `sharing-equally`

**Able to:** Share a total equally between a known number of people, write the division fact, and check it with a multiplication receipt.

**The idea that carries it:** Sharing asks "how many each?" — and the answer is right only if multiplying it back by the number of sharers returns the total you started with.

**Where students break** — the English warns about these, and they are
worth keeping however you phrase them:
- Answering with the number of sharers instead of the share — saying 12 ÷ 3 = 3.
- Calling an uneven split a division — sharing 12 as 5, 4 and 3.

**2 worked examples.** Yours to choose — different numbers, a
different context, a Mongolian setting instead of an American one. What
each must *do* is fixed:
- `g3dv-l1-we1` — Share $12$ buuz equally between $3$ children. How many each?
- `g3dv-l1-we2` — Share $20$ sweets equally between $4$ children.

**2 try-it problems**, same freedom and same condition.

### 2. `grouping`

**Able to:** Group a total into bundles of a known size, write the division fact, and tell a grouping question apart from a sharing one.

**The idea that carries it:** Sharing knows the number of parts and finds their size; grouping knows the size and finds the number of parts — and the same division fact answers both.

**Where students break** — the English warns about these, and they are
worth keeping however you phrase them:
- Reading "bags of 3" as "3 bags" and answering 12 ÷ 3 as though there were three bundles of four.
- Thinking sharing and grouping need different division facts.

**2 worked examples.** Yours to choose — different numbers, a
different context, a Mongolian setting instead of an American one. What
each must *do* is fixed:
- `g3dv-l2-we1` — Twelve buuz are packed into bags of $3$. How many bags?
- `g3dv-l2-we2` — Twenty sweets are packed into bags of $5$. How many bags — and how does this differ from sharing $20$ between $5$?

**2 try-it problems**, same freedom and same condition.

### 3. `division-facts-from-the-tables`

**Able to:** Read division facts out of the tables of $2$, $5$ and $10$, write the four facts of a multiplication–division family, and use them to divide quickly.

**The idea that carries it:** Division facts are not new facts. Each one is a times fact read backwards, so the tables you already know answer every division you meet.

**Where students break** — the English warns about these, and they are
worth keeping however you phrase them:
- Turning a division round — answering 20 ÷ 5 as 5 ÷ 20 or claiming both are the same.
- Expecting an odd number to halve exactly, e.g. 15 ÷ 2 = 7.

**2 worked examples.** Yours to choose — different numbers, a
different context, a Mongolian setting instead of an American one. What
each must *do* is fixed:
- `g3dv-l3-we1` — Write the four facts of the family for $4$, $5$ and $20$.
- `g3dv-l3-we2` — Find $30 \div 5$ and $30 \div 10$, and say how the two answers are related.

**2 try-it problems**, same freedom and same condition.

### 4. `dividing-by-3-and-4`

**Able to:** Divide by $3$ using the three times table, divide by $4$ by halving twice, and recognise which numbers divide exactly by each.

**The idea that carries it:** Divide by $3$ by counting threes, and by $4$ by halving twice — and check every answer by multiplying it back.

**Where students break** — the English warns about these, and they are
worth keeping however you phrase them:
- Halving only once when dividing by 4 — answering 24 ÷ 4 = 12.
- Forcing a division that does not come out, such as 22 ÷ 4 = 5.

**2 worked examples.** Yours to choose — different numbers, a
different context, a Mongolian setting instead of an American one. What
each must *do* is fixed:
- `g3dv-l4-we1` — Find $24 \div 3$ and prove it with the receipt.
- `g3dv-l4-we2` — Find $24 \div 4$ by halving twice.

**2 try-it problems**, same freedom and same condition.

### 5. `choosing-the-operation`

**Able to:** Decide from a story whether to multiply or divide, by working out which of the three numbers in the family is missing.

**The idea that carries it:** Find which of the three — groups, group size, total — the story leaves out. Missing total, multiply; missing group, divide.

**Where students break** — the English warns about these, and they are
worth keeping however you phrase them:
- Multiplying whenever two numbers appear, so "20 jars on 5 shelves" becomes 100.
- Ignoring the size of the answer — giving a share bigger than the total.

**2 worked examples.** Yours to choose — different numbers, a
different context, a Mongolian setting instead of an American one. What
each must *do* is fixed:
- `g3dv-l5-we1` — Five shelves hold $4$ jars each. How many jars? Say which number was missing.
- `g3dv-l5-we2` — Twenty jars are shared equally between $5$ shelves. How many on each?

**2 try-it problems**, same freedom and same condition.

## What you owe for every example you change

A worked example or try-it problem with different numbers needs a new
**check** — a line of maths that says what must be true, so we can verify it
mechanically without a second person re-solving it.

**You do not write these.** Write the example and its answer in plain
Mongolian, and Build turns it into the assertion. If an example's answer
cannot be stated exactly, the example is not ready.

**Practice and test-yourself problems are different: do not change their
numbers.** Those are graded against a stored answer key and every student's
history is keyed to them. Rewrite the *wording* freely; leave the maths.

### Careful — English uses one word where Mongolian uses two

These are the ones that go wrong silently. The word above is the sense
this topic most likely means; check it against what you are actually
writing, and say so if the topic needs the other one.

**count** — proposed **тоолох**

> POLYSEMOUS. Verb "to count" = тоолох (ministry 12.14, and shipped throughout). Noun "the count" = тоо when it means how many items («Mean = sum ÷ count» → «Дундаж = нийлбэр ÷ тоо»), but тооллого when it means the tally/inventory itself («the small raised exponent is the count» → «илтгэгч нь тооллого»; "take inventory" → «Тооллого хий»). This entry proposes the verb. Do not write тоолол.

**size** — proposed **хэмжээ**

> POLYSEMOUS in one narrow place. хэмжээ is the general word for size/magnitude — ministry for the size of a matrix, shipped for the size of a number, a piece, a group — and is what this entry proposes; the productive forms are ижил хэмжээтэй (of the same size) and ижил хэмжээний + noun. But clothing/shoe size is размер in shipped material («Гутлын размер: $36, 38, ...$», "shoe size and quiz score" → «Гутлын размер ба шалгалтын оноо»), so a data-handling example about shoe sizes keeps размер. Note хэмжээ is also the word this batch's "amount" lands on — that collision is real and intended, do not invent a second word to separate them.

## How it should sound

**«та», formal.** A student on this product is a customer, and «чи» is not
suitable for one. Formal is not the same as stiff — the English has jokes and
energy, and that should survive. Do not flatten it into textbook prose.

Quotes take «...». Proper names take their standard Mongolian forms. Product
names and symbols stay Latin.

> Note if you have seen earlier material: grades 6 and 7 are written in «чи»,
> which is now the wrong register. Do not copy their tone.

## Writing maths

Only the rules that change what you type:

1. **Do not put Mongolian words inside a formula.** Write the formula in
   symbols and the words outside it. (If a word truly must sit inside one,
   mark it and Build will wrap it correctly.)
2. **Letters stay Latin** — `x`, `h`, point names, function names — even in
   Mongolian prose. Gloss one on first use if it helps.
3. **Bold is `**like this**`.** Single asterisks do not work and will appear
   on screen as asterisks.
4. **Units:** см, кг, мл read fine in a sentence.
