## What this topic must teach

A student finishes this topic holding these ideas. Everything else in the
brief serves them.

1. **The line is the boundary; the shading is the answer.** Students draw the line and stop. The solution is a region, and half the marks live in choosing which side.

2. **Test one point.** The origin, whenever it is not on the line. One substitution settles the shading with no reasoning about direction — reliable and fast.

3. **Solid or dashed says whether the boundary counts.** ≤ and ≥ include it, < and > do not. A small mark carrying real meaning.

**The error to design against:** Flipping the shading after rearranging the inequality to y = form, because the rearrangement multiplied by a negative and the sign was not reversed.

If your Mongolian version lands those and a student can do the practice
set, the topic is right — however you got there.

Each lesson, and what the student must end up able to do:

### 1. `solutions-in-the-plane`

**Able to:** Test whether points satisfy two-variable inequalities and understand solutions as regions.

**The idea that carries it:** Substitute the point; true means inside the solution region. Inequalities claim half the plane.

**Where students break** — the English warns about these, and they are
worth keeping however you phrase them:
- Substituting the coordinates into the wrong letters.
- Counting boundary points as solutions of strict inequalities.

**3 worked examples.** Yours to choose — different numbers, a
different context, a Mongolian setting instead of an American one. What
each must *do* is fixed:
- `sys1-we1` — Is $(1, 5)$ a solution of $y > 2x + 1$? Is $(2, 3)$?
- `sys1-we2` — Is $(4, 2)$ a solution of $3x - 2y \le 8$?
- `sys1-we3` — Which of $(0, 0)$, $(5, 1)$, $(2, 6)$ solve $x + y < 6$?

**2 try-it problems**, same freedom and same condition.

### 2. `graphing-half-planes`

**Able to:** Graph two-variable inequalities: boundary line (solid/dashed), test point, and shading.

**The idea that carries it:** Fence (solid/dashed), test one point, paint the true side.

**Where students break** — the English warns about these, and they are
worth keeping however you phrase them:
- Always shading above for > without isolating y.
- Testing a point that's ON the boundary.

**3 worked examples.** Yours to choose — different numbers, a
different context, a Mongolian setting instead of an American one. What
each must *do* is fixed:
- `sys2-we1` — Describe the graph of $y \le 2x - 1$.
- `sys2-we2` — Describe the graph of $x + y > 4$.
- `sys2-we3` — Describe the graph of $x \ge -2$.

**2 try-it problems**, same freedom and same condition.

### 3. `systems-of-inequalities`

**Able to:** Solve systems of linear inequalities graphically and verify points against every condition.

**The idea that carries it:** Graph every half-plane; the system's solutions live where all the shadings overlap.

**Where students break** — the English warns about these, and they are
worth keeping however you phrase them:
- Accepting a point that passes MOST conditions.
- Shading the union instead of the intersection.

**3 worked examples.** Yours to choose — different numbers, a
different context, a Mongolian setting instead of an American one. What
each must *do* is fixed:
- `sys3-we1` — Is $(2, 3)$ a solution of the system $y > x$ and $x + y < 8$?
- `sys3-we2` — Is $(5, 6)$ in the same system?
- `sys3-we3` — Find the corner of the region bounded by $y = x$ and $x + y = 8$.

**2 try-it problems**, same freedom and same condition.

### 4. `writing-constraint-systems`

**Able to:** Translate multi-constraint stories into systems of inequalities, including natural nonnegativity constraints.

**The idea that carries it:** One inequality per resource or requirement, plus nonnegativity — the story's rulebook in algebra.

**Where students break** — the English warns about these, and they are
worth keeping however you phrase them:
- Forgetting the nonnegativity pair.
- Flipping capacity and requirement directions.

**3 worked examples.** Yours to choose — different numbers, a
different context, a Mongolian setting instead of an American one. What
each must *do* is fixed:
- `sys4-we1` — Write the bake-sale system: eggs ($2$/brownie batch, $1$/cookie batch, $24$ total), oven ($20$ and $15$ min, $300$ total).
- `sys4-we2` — Check: can the sale make $6$ brownie batches and $8$ cookie batches?
- `sys4-we3` — A trainer schedules cardio ($c$) and weights ($w$) hours: at least $3$ h cardio, at least $2$ h weights, at most $10$ h total. Write the system.

**2 try-it problems**, same freedom and same condition.

### 5. `feasible-regions`

**Able to:** Graph feasible regions from constraint systems and find their corner points.

**The idea that carries it:** The feasible region is the constraints' overlap; its corners come from solving boundary pairs.

**Where students break** — the English warns about these, and they are
worth keeping however you phrase them:
- Reporting fence intersections that aren't corners.
- Solving corner systems by guessing from the sketch.

**3 worked examples.** Yours to choose — different numbers, a
different context, a Mongolian setting instead of an American one. What
each must *do* is fixed:
- `sys5-we1` — Find the corners of: $x \ge 0$, $y \ge 0$, $x + y \le 6$.
- `sys5-we2` — Find the corners of: $x \ge 0$, $y \ge 0$, $2x + y \le 8$, $x + 2y \le 10$.
- `sys5-we3` — Is the region of $x \ge 1$ and $y \ge x$ bounded or unbounded?

**2 try-it problems**, same freedom and same condition.

### 6. `best-point-in-the-region`

**Able to:** Evaluate an objective at a feasible region's corners to find maximum or minimum plans.

**The idea that carries it:** Score the corners, crown the best — linear objectives peak at vertices.

**Where students break** — the English warns about these, and they are
worth keeping however you phrase them:
- Evaluating the objective at interior points 'to be safe'.
- Optimizing over crossings outside the region.

**3 worked examples.** Yours to choose — different numbers, a
different context, a Mongolian setting instead of an American one. What
each must *do* is fixed:
- `sys6-we1` — Bake sale: corners $(0,0), (12,0), (6,12), (0,20)$; profit $P = 9x + 5y$. Find the best plan.
- `sys6-we2` — Same region, new prices: $P = 10x + 5y$. Now what?
- `sys6-we3` — Minimize cost $C = 4x + 3y$ over corners $(2,1), (3,1), (2,2)$.

**2 try-it problems**, same freedom and same condition.

## What you owe for every example you change

A worked example or try-it problem with different numbers needs a new
**check** — a line of maths that says what must be true, so we can verify it
mechanically without a second person re-solving it.

**You do not write these.** Write the example and its answer in plain
Mongolian, and Build turns it into the assertion. If an example's answer
cannot be stated exactly, the example is not ready.

**Practice and test-yourself problems are different: do not change their
numbers.** Those are graded against a stored answer key and every student's
history is keyed to them. Rewrite the *wording* freely; leave the maths.

### Careful — English uses one word where Mongolian uses two

These are the ones that go wrong silently. The word above is the sense
this topic most likely means; check it against what you are actually
writing, and say so if the topic needs the other one.

**half** — proposed **хагас**

> POLYSEMOUS. хагас is the noun "a half" (the quantity 1/2) and is what this entry proposes. But when "half" means one of two parts of a set or a figure, shipped Mongolian uses тал: "half at or below, half at or above" → «тал нь түүнээс дээшгүй, тал нь доошгүй»; "each exactly half the slice" → «тус бүр нь талхны яг тал»; "The triangle is exactly half of it" → «Гурвалжин чинь яг тал нь». Rule of thumb: хагас for the computed amount, тал for one of the two portions. тал is also "side" (of a shape, of an equation), so it carries real ambiguity — prefer хагас wherever the amount is meant.

**translate** — proposed **хөрвүүлэх**

> Words→symbols sense only; one shipped string instead says «болго». The geometry verb is «параллель зөөх» (MoE 10.11) and the two must not merge — same English word, two Mongolian verbs.

## How it should sound

**«та», formal.** A student on this product is a customer, and «чи» is not
suitable for one. Formal is not the same as stiff — the English has jokes and
energy, and that should survive. Do not flatten it into textbook prose.

Quotes take «...». Proper names take their standard Mongolian forms. Product
names and symbols stay Latin.

> Note if you have seen earlier material: grades 6 and 7 are written in «чи»,
> which is now the wrong register. Do not copy their tone.

## Writing maths

Only the rules that change what you type:

1. **Do not put Mongolian words inside a formula.** Write the formula in
   symbols and the words outside it. (If a word truly must sit inside one,
   mark it and Build will wrap it correctly.)
2. **Letters stay Latin** — `x`, `h`, point names, function names — even in
   Mongolian prose. Gloss one on first use if it helps.
3. **Bold is `**like this**`.** Single asterisks do not work and will appear
   on screen as asterisks.
4. **Units:** см, кг, мл read fine in a sentence.
