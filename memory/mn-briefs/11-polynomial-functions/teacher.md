## What this topic must teach

A student finishes this topic holding these ideas. Everything else in the
brief serves them.

1. **The degree and leading coefficient decide the ends.** Before any point is plotted, those two numbers tell you what the graph does far left and far right. That is the cheapest prediction in the topic and students skip it.

2. **A root is a factor is an x-intercept.** Three names for one fact. Once a student holds them as the same thing, factoring, solving and sketching stop being three skills.

3. **Multiplicity changes the shape at a root.** An odd multiplicity crosses the axis, an even one touches and turns back. That single rule is what makes a sketch from factored form possible.

**The error to design against:** Assuming a degree-n polynomial always shows n distinct x-intercepts. Repeated and complex roots both reduce the count, and the graph is the evidence.

If your Mongolian version lands those and a student can do the practice
set, the topic is right — however you got there.

Each lesson, and what the student must end up able to do:

### 1. `meet-polynomial-functions`

**Able to:** Identify a polynomial function's degree and leading coefficient, evaluate it, and count its possible turning points.

**The idea that carries it:** Degree = highest power; it caps the turning points at n − 1. Smooth, unbroken curves — no jumps, no corners.

**Where students break** — the English warns about these, and they are
worth keeping however you phrase them:
- Reading the degree off the FIRST term as written.
- Thinking degree n means EXACTLY n − 1 turns.

**3 worked examples.** Yours to choose — different numbers, a
different context, a Mongolian setting instead of an American one. What
each must *do* is fixed:
- `pf1-we1` — Degree, leading coefficient, and $f(2)$ for $f(x) = 2x^3 - 5x + 1$?
- `pf1-we2` — Evaluate $f(x) = x^4 - 3x^2 + 2$ at $x = -2$ and $x = 1$.
- `pf1-we3` — How many turning points can a quartic (degree 4) have at most? A cubic?

**2 try-it problems**, same freedom and same condition.

### 2. `end-behavior`

**Able to:** Predict a polynomial's end behavior (both arms) from the degree's parity and the leading coefficient's sign.

**The idea that carries it:** Zoomed out, only the leading term speaks: even degree → arms together; odd → arms opposite; negative flips both.

**Where students break** — the English warns about these, and they are
worth keeping however you phrase them:
- Letting a big middle term change the verdict.
- Flipping the odd-positive pattern.

**3 worked examples.** Yours to choose — different numbers, a
different context, a Mongolian setting instead of an American one. What
each must *do* is fixed:
- `pf2-we1` — End behavior of $y = x^3 - 100x^2 + 5$?
- `pf2-we2` — End behavior of $y = -3x^4 + x^3 - 7$?
- `pf2-we3` — A graph's arms both point UP. What do you know about its equation?

**2 try-it problems**, same freedom and same condition.

### 3. `zeros-and-factors`

**Able to:** Read zeros from factored form, build factored equations from zeros, and use the degree-n-has-at-most-n-zeros rule.

**The idea that carries it:** Zeros = x-intercepts = factors' deaths. Zero r ↔ factor (x − r); degree n allows at most n of them.

**Where students break** — the English warns about these, and they are
worth keeping however you phrase them:
- Reading the zeros of $(x+2)(x-3)$ as $2$ and $-3$.
- Forgetting the leading coefficient when building from zeros.

**3 worked examples.** Yours to choose — different numbers, a
different context, a Mongolian setting instead of an American one. What
each must *do* is fixed:
- `pf3-we1` — Zeros of $y = (x+2)(x-1)(x-3)$?
- `pf3-we2` — Write a cubic with zeros $-1, 2, 4$ passing through standard height (leading 1), and verify one zero.
- `pf3-we3` — Zeros of $y = x^3 - 4x$?

**2 try-it problems**, same freedom and same condition.

### 4. `multiplicity`

**Able to:** Use a zero's multiplicity to predict crossing style: odd multiplicity crosses (≥3 flattens), even multiplicity bounces.

**The idea that carries it:** Multiplicity = factor's exponent. Odd → cross (≥3 flattens first); even → bounce. Multiplicities sum to the degree.

**Where students break** — the English warns about these, and they are
worth keeping however you phrase them:
- Treating every zero as a clean crossing.
- Miscounting the degree by forgetting exponents.

**3 worked examples.** Yours to choose — different numbers, a
different context, a Mongolian setting instead of an American one. What
each must *do* is fixed:
- `pf4-we1` — For $y = (x+2)(x-1)^2$: list zeros with multiplicities and landing styles.
- `pf4-we2` — Same question for $y = x^3(x-4)$.
- `pf4-we3` — Does $y = (x-2)^2$ cross or bounce at $x = 2$? Verify with points either side.

**2 try-it problems**, same freedom and same condition.

### 5. `remainder-and-factor-theorems`

**Able to:** Use the Remainder Theorem ($f(a)$ = remainder on division by $x-a$) and Factor Theorem ($f(a)=0 \iff (x-a)$ is a factor) to test and find factors.

**The idea that carries it:** f(a) = the remainder on dividing by (x−a); f(a) = 0 means (x−a) is a factor. Test candidates, peel, repeat.

**Where students break** — the English warns about these, and they are
worth keeping however you phrase them:
- Evaluating $f(-a)$ to test the factor $(x - a)$.
- Stopping after one factor is found.

**3 worked examples.** Yours to choose — different numbers, a
different context, a Mongolian setting instead of an American one. What
each must *do* is fixed:
- `pf5-we1` — Find the remainder when $f(x) = x^3 - 2x^2 - 5x + 6$ is divided by $(x - 2)$.
- `pf5-we2` — Is $(x - 1)$ a factor of $f(x) = x^3 - 2x^2 - 5x + 6$? Fully factor if so.
- `pf5-we3` — For what $k$ is $(x + 1)$ a factor of $f(x) = x^3 + kx - 4$?

**2 try-it problems**, same freedom and same condition.

### 6. `sketching-polynomials`

**Able to:** Sketch factored polynomials with the four-step recipe: end behavior, zeros, multiplicities, y-intercept — then trace.

**The idea that carries it:** Arms → zeros → landing styles → y-intercept, then trace. Between zeros the sign is fixed — sample once per gap.

**Where students break** — the English warns about these, and they are
worth keeping however you phrase them:
- Tracing landings right but arms wrong (or vice versa).
- Letting the curve wander across the axis between zeros.

**3 worked examples.** Yours to choose — different numbers, a
different context, a Mongolian setting instead of an American one. What
each must *do* is fixed:
- `pf6-we1` — Run the recipe on $y = (x+2)(x-1)^2$.
- `pf6-we2` — Run the recipe on $y = -x(x-2)(x+3)$.
- `pf6-we3` — Sketch data for $y = (x-2)^2(x+1)^2$: arms, landings, y-intercept.

**2 try-it problems**, same freedom and same condition.

## What you owe for every example you change

A worked example or try-it problem with different numbers needs a new
**check** — a line of maths that says what must be true, so we can verify it
mechanically without a second person re-solving it.

**You do not write these.** Write the example and its answer in plain
Mongolian, and Build turns it into the assertion. If an example's answer
cannot be stated exactly, the example is not ready.

**Practice and test-yourself problems are different: do not change their
numbers.** Those are graded against a stored answer key and every student's
history is keyed to them. Rewrite the *wording* freely; leave the maths.

### Careful — English uses one word where Mongolian uses two

These are the ones that go wrong silently. The word above is the sense
this topic most likely means; check it against what you are actually
writing, and say so if the topic needs the other one.

**factor** — proposed **хуваагч**

> One English key, two live Mongolian terms: a number's factor/divisor is «хуваагч» (164× in production, not in the ministry standard), while an algebraic factor is «үржигдэхүүн» (ministry 10.2 «үржигдэхүүн болгон задлах»; 80× in production). Needs two keys — and note «хуваагдагч» (105×) is the multiple/dividend, easy to confuse.

**count** — proposed **тоолох**

> POLYSEMOUS. Verb "to count" = тоолох (ministry 12.14, and shipped throughout). Noun "the count" = тоо when it means how many items («Mean = sum ÷ count» → «Дундаж = нийлбэр ÷ тоо»), but тооллого when it means the tally/inventory itself («the small raised exponent is the count» → «илтгэгч нь тооллого»; "take inventory" → «Тооллого хий»). This entry proposes the verb. Do not write тоолол.

## How it should sound

**«та», formal.** A student on this product is a customer, and «чи» is not
suitable for one. Formal is not the same as stiff — the English has jokes and
energy, and that should survive. Do not flatten it into textbook prose.

Quotes take «...». Proper names take their standard Mongolian forms. Product
names and symbols stay Latin.

> Note if you have seen earlier material: grades 6 and 7 are written in «чи»,
> which is now the wrong register. Do not copy their tone.

## Writing maths

Only the rules that change what you type:

1. **Do not put Mongolian words inside a formula.** Write the formula in
   symbols and the words outside it. (If a word truly must sit inside one,
   mark it and Build will wrap it correctly.)
2. **Letters stay Latin** — `x`, `h`, point names, function names — even in
   Mongolian prose. Gloss one on first use if it helps.
3. **Bold is `**like this**`.** Single asterisks do not work and will appear
   on screen as asterisks.
4. **Units:** см, кг, мл read fine in a sentence.
