Each lesson, and what the student must end up able to do:

### 1. `naming-flat-shapes`

**Able to:** Name a circle, triangle, square and rectangle, say what makes each one different, and pick a shape out of a picture by its name.

**The idea that carries it:** Flat shapes are named by their straight sides: three for a triangle, four for a square or rectangle, and none at all for a circle.

**Where students break** — the English warns about these, and they are
worth keeping however you phrase them:
- Only recognising a triangle when it points upwards and sits flat.
- Saying a square is not a rectangle.

**2 worked examples.** Yours to choose — different numbers, a
different context, a Mongolian setting instead of an American one. What
each must *do* is fixed:
- `g3sh-l1-we1` — A shape has three straight sides. What is it called, and how many corners does it have?
- `g3sh-l1-we2` — What is the difference between a square and a rectangle?

**2 try-it problems**, same freedom and same condition.

### 2. `square-corners`

**Able to:** Test a corner for squareness by matching it against a known square corner, and use square corners to tell rectangles from other four-sided shapes.

**The idea that carries it:** A square corner is the corner of a page, and a rectangle is a four-sided shape with four of them.

**Where students break** — the English warns about these, and they are
worth keeping however you phrase them:
- Calling any four-sided shape a rectangle.
- Deciding a corner is square by eye, from across the desk.

**2 worked examples.** Yours to choose — different numbers, a
different context, a Mongolian setting instead of an American one. What
each must *do* is fixed:
- `g3sh-l2-we1` — How can you check whether a table corner is a square corner, without measuring?
- `g3sh-l2-we2` — A four-sided shape leans over like a pushed box. Is it a rectangle?

**2 try-it problems**, same freedom and same condition.

### 3. `solid-shapes`

**Able to:** Name the cube, sphere, cylinder and cone, count the flat faces of each, and predict whether a solid will roll, stack or slide.

**The idea that carries it:** Flat faces let a solid stack; curved surfaces let it roll — and a solid with both can do either, depending on which part is touching the table.

**Where students break** — the English warns about these, and they are
worth keeping however you phrase them:
- Deciding a solid either rolls or stacks, never both.
- Calling a ball a circle.

**2 worked examples.** Yours to choose — different numbers, a
different context, a Mongolian setting instead of an American one. What
each must *do* is fixed:
- `g3sh-l3-we1` — How many flat faces has a cube, and what does that let it do?
- `g3sh-l3-we2` — A tin of tea both rolls and stands up. Explain how.

**2 try-it problems**, same freedom and same condition.

### 4. `shape-patterns`

**Able to:** Find the repeating unit in a shape pattern, continue the pattern, and tell a repeating pattern from a growing one.

**The idea that carries it:** Find the piece that repeats and count its length — that is all you need to continue a pattern or say what sits at any position.

**Where students break** — the English warns about these, and they are
worth keeping however you phrase them:
- Taking the repeating piece to be longer than it is — calling "triangle, square, triangle, square" a four-shape repeat.
- Calling a growing pattern a repeating one.

**2 worked examples.** Yours to choose — different numbers, a
different context, a Mongolian setting instead of an American one. What
each must *do* is fixed:
- `g3sh-l4-we1` — A border goes triangle, square, triangle, square, triangle. What is the repeating piece, and what comes next?
- `g3sh-l4-we2` — A pattern goes circle, square, triangle, circle, square, triangle. How long is the repeat, and what is the $7$th shape?

**2 try-it problems**, same freedom and same condition.

### 5. `sorting-shapes`

**Able to:** Sort shapes by a stated rule, describe the rule a sorting used, and see that one shape can belong to different groups under different rules.

**The idea that carries it:** A sorting is only as clear as its rule — state the rule first, and remember that a different rule will move the shapes into different piles.

**Where students break** — the English warns about these, and they are
worth keeping however you phrase them:
- Sorting without saying what the rule is, so nobody can check the piles.
- Thinking a shape belongs to only one group forever.

**2 worked examples.** Yours to choose — different numbers, a
different context, a Mongolian setting instead of an American one. What
each must *do* is fixed:
- `g3sh-l5-we1` — Sort a triangle, a square, a rectangle and a circle by number of sides. Which piles result?
- `g3sh-l5-we2` — Now sort the same four shapes by "are all the sides equal?". What changes?

**2 try-it problems**, same freedom and same condition.

## What you owe for every example you change

A worked example or try-it problem with different numbers needs a new
**check** — a line of maths that says what must be true, so we can verify it
mechanically without a second person re-solving it.

**You do not write these.** Write the example and its answer in plain
Mongolian, and Build turns it into the assertion. If an example's answer
cannot be stated exactly, the example is not ready.

**Practice and test-yourself problems are different: do not change their
numbers.** Those are graded against a stored answer key and every student's
history is keyed to them. Rewrite the *wording* freely; leave the maths.

### Careful — English uses one word where Mongolian uses two

These are the ones that go wrong silently. The word above is the sense
this topic most likely means; check it against what you are actually
writing, and say so if the topic needs the other one.

**shape** — proposed **дүрс**

> Production splits the word by sense: «дүрс» for a geometric shape (73×) and «хэлбэр» for the shape of a distribution ("Center, spread, shape" → «Төв, тархалт, хэлбэр»). «хэлбэр» is also this site's word for "form". Needs two keys — geometric shape vs. distribution shape.

**count** — proposed **тоолох**

> POLYSEMOUS. Verb "to count" = тоолох (ministry 12.14, and shipped throughout). Noun "the count" = тоо when it means how many items («Mean = sum ÷ count» → «Дундаж = нийлбэр ÷ тоо»), but тооллого when it means the tally/inventory itself («the small raised exponent is the count» → «илтгэгч нь тооллого»; "take inventory" → «Тооллого хий»). This entry proposes the verb. Do not write тоолол.

**Section headings** are fixed too, because the shipped lessons already use
them and a student should meet the same words in every lesson:

**Бодсон жишээнүүд** · **Өөрөө туршиж үз** · **Түргэн шалгалт** · **Тоглож үз** · **Сонирхолтой баримт** · **Эргэн дүгнэлт** · **Юу сурснаа эргэн харъя**

Introducing a term that is not here? Tell us — it goes in the glossary so the
next topic uses the same word.

## How it should sound

**«та», formal.** A student on this product is a customer, and «чи» is not
suitable for one. Formal is not the same as stiff — the English has jokes and
energy, and that should survive. Do not flatten it into textbook prose.

Quotes take «...». Proper names take their standard Mongolian forms. Product
names and symbols stay Latin.

> Note if you have seen earlier material: grades 6 and 7 are written in «чи»,
> which is now the wrong register. Do not copy their tone.

## Writing maths

Only the rules that change what you type:

1. **Do not put Mongolian words inside a formula.** Write the formula in
   symbols and the words outside it. (If a word truly must sit inside one,
   mark it and Build will wrap it correctly.)
2. **Letters stay Latin** — `x`, `h`, point names, function names — even in
   Mongolian prose. Gloss one on first use if it helps.
3. **Bold is `**like this**`.** Single asterisks do not work and will appear
   on screen as asterisks.
4. **Units:** см, кг, мл read fine in a sentence.
