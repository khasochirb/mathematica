Each lesson, and what the student must end up able to do:

### 1. `equal-groups`

**Able to:** Read a picture of equal groups as a times fact, write a times fact as repeated addition, and draw the picture that a times fact describes.

**The idea that carries it:** A times fact is repeated addition in one word: the first number counts the equal groups, the second counts inside one group — and the picture and the fact must always tell the same story.

**Where students break** — the English warns about these, and they are
worth keeping however you phrase them:
- Adding the two numbers — reading 4 × 3 as 4 + 3 = 7.
- Writing a times fact for unequal groups — calling groups of 3, 3 and 4 "3 × 3".

**2 worked examples.** Yours to choose — different numbers, a
different context, a Mongolian setting instead of an American one. What
each must *do* is fixed:
- `g4tm-l1-we1` — Four gers each have $3$ horses tied outside. Write the repeated addition and the times fact for the total.
- `g4tm-l1-we2` — Batu packs $6$ bags with $5$ buuz in each. How many buuz — as a sum and as a times fact?

**2 try-it problems**, same freedom and same condition.

### 2. `tables-2-5-and-10`

**Able to:** Master the tables of 2, 5 and 10 through count-bys and doubles, and use their endings patterns — times-5 answers end in 0 or 5, times-10 answers end in 0 — to catch wrong answers on sight.

**The idea that carries it:** The tables of 2, 5 and 10 are skip-counts you already carry: doubles, hands of five, whole tens — and their endings patterns work as an alarm that catches wrong answers on sight.

**Where students break** — the English warns about these, and they are
worth keeping however you phrase them:
- Treating ×5 like ×10 — writing 7 × 5 = 75 by attaching the 5.
- Losing count mid skip-count — reaching "6 × 5" by saying 5, 10, 15, 20, 25.

**2 worked examples.** Yours to choose — different numbers, a
different context, a Mongolian setting instead of an American one. What
each must *do* is fixed:
- `g4tm-l2-we1` — Find $6 \times 2$ and $9 \times 2$ using doubles.
- `g4tm-l2-we2` — Find $8 \times 5$ and $8 \times 10$, and compare them.

**2 try-it problems**, same freedom and same condition.

### 3. `tables-6-to-9`

**Able to:** Build the tables of 6 to 9 from owned facts — sixes from fives, sevens from fives and twos, eights and nines from tens — and use the 9s digit patterns as a check.

**The idea that carries it:** Build the fact you need from a fact you already own: sixes from fives, sevens from fives and twos, eights and nines from tens — and let the 9s digit pattern stand guard over the answers.

**Where students break** — the English warns about these, and they are
worth keeping however you phrase them:
- Adding the wrong extra — building 7 × 6 as 7 × 5 + 5 = 40.
- Trusting the 9s digit-sum alone — answering 6 × 9 = 45 "because 4 + 5 = 9".

**2 worked examples.** Yours to choose — different numbers, a
different context, a Mongolian setting instead of an American one. What
each must *do* is fixed:
- `g4tm-l3-we1` — Find $7 \times 6$ starting from $7 \times 5$.
- `g4tm-l3-we2` — Find $8 \times 9$ from $8 \times 10$, then check it with the digits pattern.

**2 try-it problems**, same freedom and same condition.

### 4. `arrays-and-turnaround`

**Able to:** Read an array as rows and as columns to see that the two readings give one total, use the turnaround a × b = b × a to get partner facts free, and count how much of the table is left to memorise.

**The idea that carries it:** An array read as rows and read as columns is the same count — so a × b = b × a always, every fact has a free partner, and the table you must memorise is barely half as big as it looks.

**Where students break** — the English warns about these, and they are
worth keeping however you phrase them:
- Counting an array twice — 4 rows of 6 makes 24, "plus" 6 columns of 4 makes 48.
- Hoping turnaround erases all memorising — "I'll just flip everything".

**2 worked examples.** Yours to choose — different numbers, a
different context, a Mongolian setting instead of an American one. What
each must *do* is fixed:
- `g4tm-l4-we1` — A tray holds $4$ rows of $6$ buuz. Count it as rows and as columns, and write both facts.
- `g4tm-l4-we2` — You know $2 \times 9 = 18$. Find $9 \times 2$ without any new work, and say why that is allowed.

**2 try-it problems**, same freedom and same condition.

### 5. `multiply-bigger-numbers`

**Able to:** Multiply a two-digit number by a one-digit number by splitting it into tens and ones, keep the tens' place value honest, and solve simple money and word problems with a tens fence as the sanity check.

**The idea that carries it:** Split the two-digit number by place, multiply each piece with a table fact you own, and add the pieces back — with a tens fence around the answer to catch any escape.

**Where students break** — the English warns about these, and they are
worth keeping however you phrase them:
- Multiplying only the tens — 23 × 4 = 80.
- Gluing piece answers side by side — 37 × 5 from pieces "15" and "35" glued into 1535.

**2 worked examples.** Yours to choose — different numbers, a
different context, a Mongolian setting instead of an American one. What
each must *do* is fixed:
- `g4tm-l5-we1` — Find $23 \times 4$ by splitting into tens and ones.
- `g4tm-l5-we2` — A pencil costs $85$ togrog. Find the cost of $6$ pencils.

**2 try-it problems**, same freedom and same condition.

**check** — a line of maths that says what must be true, so we can verify it
mechanically without a second person re-solving it.

cannot be stated exactly, the example is not ready.

**Practice and test-yourself problems are different: do not change their
numbers.** Those are graded against a stored answer key and every student's
history is keyed to them. Rewrite the *wording* freely; leave the maths.

### Careful — English uses one word where Mongolian uses two

These are the ones that go wrong silently. The word above is the sense
this topic most likely means; check it against what you are actually
writing, and say so if the topic needs the other one.

**count** — proposed **тоолох**

> POLYSEMOUS. Verb "to count" = тоолох (ministry 12.14, and shipped throughout). Noun "the count" = тоо when it means how many items («Mean = sum ÷ count» → «Дундаж = нийлбэр ÷ тоо»), but тооллого when it means the tally/inventory itself («the small raised exponent is the count» → «илтгэгч нь тооллого»; "take inventory" → «Тооллого хий»). This entry proposes the verb. Do not write тоолол.

**half** — proposed **хагас**

> POLYSEMOUS. хагас is the noun "a half" (the quantity 1/2) and is what this entry proposes. But when "half" means one of two parts of a set or a figure, shipped Mongolian uses тал: "half at or below, half at or above" → «тал нь түүнээс дээшгүй, тал нь доошгүй»; "each exactly half the slice" → «тус бүр нь талхны яг тал»; "The triangle is exactly half of it" → «Гурвалжин чинь яг тал нь». Rule of thumb: хагас for the computed amount, тал for one of the two portions. тал is also "side" (of a shape, of an equation), so it carries real ambiguity — prefer хагас wherever the amount is meant.

**tens** — proposed **аравт**

> POLYSEMOUS. As a place value, tens = аравт and "the tens place" = аравтын орон — attested both in the shipped mirror and in the ЭШ papers, which is as settled as this family gets. Two traps. (a) The genitive аравтын also heads аравтын бутархай = decimal fraction, so «аравтын орон» (tens place) and «аравтын бутархайн орон» (decimal place) sit one word apart — always keep бутархай when you mean the decimal. (b) "tens of thousands" and similar quantity phrases are not аравт but хэдэн арван: shipped "already tens of thousands" → «аль хэдийн хэдэн арван мянга».

## How it should sound

**«та», formal.** A student on this product is a customer, and «чи» is not
suitable for one. Formal is not the same as stiff — the English has jokes and
energy, and that should survive. Do not flatten it into textbook prose.

Quotes take «...». Proper names take their standard Mongolian forms. Product
names and symbols stay Latin.

> Note if you have seen earlier material: grades 6 and 7 are written in «чи»,
> which is now the wrong register. Do not copy their tone.

## Writing maths

Only the rules that change what you type:

1. **Do not put Mongolian words inside a formula.** Write the formula in
   symbols and the words outside it. (If a word truly must sit inside one,
   mark it and Build will wrap it correctly.)
2. **Letters stay Latin** — `x`, `h`, point names, function names — even in
   Mongolian prose. Gloss one on first use if it helps.
3. **Bold is `**like this**`.** Single asterisks do not work and will appear
   on screen as asterisks.
4. **Units:** см, кг, мл read fine in a sentence.
