## What this topic must teach

A student finishes this topic holding these ideas. Everything else in the
brief serves them.

1. **i is defined, not discovered.** We declare a number whose square is −1 because the real line cannot solve x² = −1. Nothing mystical follows — every operation on complex numbers is ordinary algebra with one substitution rule, i² = −1.

2. **Arithmetic is just collecting like terms.** Real parts with real, imaginary with imaginary. Multiplication is the distributive law plus i² = −1. Students who see this stop treating complex numbers as a separate subject.

3. **The conjugate exists to make a real denominator.** Multiplying by the conjugate clears i from the bottom exactly as multiplying by a radical conjugate clears a surd. Same move, twice.

**The error to design against:** Writing √(−4)·√(−9) = √36 = 6. The product rule for radicals holds only for non-negative numbers; the correct answer is −6. Worth one deliberate counterexample.

If your Mongolian version lands those and a student can do the practice
set, the topic is right — however you got there.

Each lesson, and what the student must end up able to do:

### 1. `meet-i`

**Able to:** Define $i$ by $i^2 = -1$, simplify square roots of negatives, and compute powers of $i$ using the 4-cycle.

**The idea that carries it:** i² = −1 and nothing else changes. √(−n) = i√n; powers of i cycle i, −1, −i, 1 — remainder mod 4 decides.

**Where students break** — the English warns about these, and they are
worth keeping however you phrase them:
- Using $\sqrt{a}\sqrt{b} = \sqrt{ab}$ on two negatives.
- Treating $i$ like a variable that can equal anything.

**3 worked examples.** Yours to choose — different numbers, a
different context, a Mongolian setting instead of an American one. What
each must *do* is fixed:
- `cx1-we1` — Simplify $\sqrt{-9}$ and $\sqrt{-50}$.
- `cx1-we2` — Compute $i^3$, $i^4$, and $i^{37}$.
- `cx1-we3` — Solve $x^2 = -25$.

**2 try-it problems**, same freedom and same condition.

### 2. `complex-numbers-and-arithmetic`

**Able to:** Identify real and imaginary parts, and add, subtract, and scale complex numbers by combining like parts.

**The idea that carries it:** z = a + bi: two parts that never blend. Combine componentwise; equality matches both parts separately.

**Where students break** — the English warns about these, and they are
worth keeping however you phrase them:
- Blending the parts: $(2+3i) + (4-5i) = 4i$ or similar mashups.
- Calling the imaginary part of $3 - 7i$ '$-7i$'.

**3 worked examples.** Yours to choose — different numbers, a
different context, a Mongolian setting instead of an American one. What
each must *do* is fixed:
- `cx2-we1` — Compute $(2+3i) + (4-5i)$ and $(2+3i) - (4-5i)$.
- `cx2-we2` — Compute $3(2-4i) - 2(1-3i)$.
- `cx2-we3` — Find real $x, y$ with $x + yi = (3+2i) + (1-5i)$.

**2 try-it problems**, same freedom and same condition.

### 3. `multiplying-complex-numbers`

**Able to:** Multiply complex numbers by expanding and converting $i^2 = -1$, including squares and the special conjugate product.

**The idea that carries it:** Expand like polynomials, then i² → −1 (jumping to the real ledger). Conjugate pairs multiply to a² + b², real.

**Where students break** — the English warns about these, and they are
worth keeping however you phrase them:
- Leaving $i^2$ in the answer.
- Computing $(3+2i)^2$ as $9 + 4i^2 = 5$.

**3 worked examples.** Yours to choose — different numbers, a
different context, a Mongolian setting instead of an American one. What
each must *do* is fixed:
- `cx3-we1` — Compute $(1+2i)(3-i)$.
- `cx3-we2` — Compute $(3+2i)^2$.
- `cx3-we3` — Compute $(4+3i)(4-3i)$.

**2 try-it problems**, same freedom and same condition.

### 4. `conjugates-and-division`

**Able to:** Use the conjugate $\bar{z} = a - bi$ and modulus to divide complex numbers, landing answers in clean $a + bi$ form.

**The idea that carries it:** Conjugate flips the i-sign; z·z̄ = a² + b² = |z|². Divide by multiplying with the bottom's conjugate.

**Where students break** — the English warns about these, and they are
worth keeping however you phrase them:
- Conjugating BOTH the top and the bottom.
- Forgetting that $|z|$ uses a PLUS.

**3 worked examples.** Yours to choose — different numbers, a
different context, a Mongolian setting instead of an American one. What
each must *do* is fixed:
- `cx4-we1` — Find $\bar{z}$ and $|z|$ for $z = 3+4i$.
- `cx4-we2` — Compute $\frac{3+i}{1-i}$.
- `cx4-we3` — Compute $\frac{10}{2+i}$.

**2 try-it problems**, same freedom and same condition.

### 5. `complex-roots-of-quadratics`

**Able to:** Solve quadratics with negative discriminants, getting conjugate-pair roots, and verify them by substitution.

**The idea that carries it:** D < 0: √D = i√|D| and the formula continues. Roots come in conjugate pairs; every quadratic has exactly 2 roots.

**Where students break** — the English warns about these, and they are
worth keeping however you phrase them:
- Still writing 'no solution' when $D < 0$.
- Losing the ÷2a on the imaginary part.

**3 worked examples.** Yours to choose — different numbers, a
different context, a Mongolian setting instead of an American one. What
each must *do* is fixed:
- `cx5-we1` — Solve $x^2 + 2x + 5 = 0$.
- `cx5-we2` — Solve $x^2 - 4x + 13 = 0$.
- `cx5-we3` — Build the quadratic (leading 1) whose roots are $3 \pm i$.

**2 try-it problems**, same freedom and same condition.

### 6. `the-complex-plane`

**Able to:** Plot complex numbers as points, read modulus as distance from the origin, and see conjugation and multiplication-by-i geometrically.

**The idea that carries it:** a + bi = the point (a, b): modulus is distance (Pythagoras), conjugation is mirror-flip, ×i is a 90° turn.

**Where students break** — the English warns about these, and they are
worth keeping however you phrase them:
- Plotting $a + bi$ at $(b, a)$.
- Expecting $\times i$ to stretch the number.

**3 worked examples.** Yours to choose — different numbers, a
different context, a Mongolian setting instead of an American one. What
each must *do* is fixed:
- `cx6-we1` — Plot-data: where do $3+4i$, $-2+i$, and $-3i$ sit, and how far is each from the origin?
- `cx6-we2` — Rotate $z = 3+4i$ by $90°$ (multiply by $i$), and check the distance survived.
- `cx6-we3` — Apply $\times i$ four times to $2 + i$ and watch it come home.

**2 try-it problems**, same freedom and same condition.

## What you owe for every example you change

A worked example or try-it problem with different numbers needs a new
**check** — a line of maths that says what must be true, so we can verify it
mechanically without a second person re-solving it.

**You do not write these.** Write the example and its answer in plain
Mongolian, and Build turns it into the assertion. If an example's answer
cannot be stated exactly, the example is not ready.

**Practice and test-yourself problems are different: do not change their
numbers.** Those are graded against a stored answer key and every student's
history is keyed to them. Rewrite the *wording* freely; leave the maths.

### Careful — English uses one word where Mongolian uses two

These are the ones that go wrong silently. The word above is the sense
this topic most likely means; check it against what you are actually
writing, and say so if the topic needs the other one.

**scale** — proposed **томсгох**

> POLYSEMOUS — three different Mongolian words, so state which you mean. (1) The verb "to scale (up)" = томсгох, which this entry proposes: it is dominant across the ratios, percent and decimal-division units. Its opposite is багасгах (scale down), and shipped once writes өсгөх instead («scale up from a friendly percent» → «нөхөрсөг хувиас өсгө») — a stray; prefer томсгох. (2) The noun "scale" of a map or a scale factor = масштаб: «масштабын коэффициент» = scale factor, «$1:1$ масштабтай» = at scale 1:1. (3) A balance scale = жинлүүр («Тэнцвэрийн жинлүүрийг төсөөл»). Confidence is medium only because of this three-way split, not because томсгох is in doubt.

**modulus** — proposed **модул**

> The bracket notation. Distinct from «абсолют утга», the value.

## How it should sound

**«та», formal.** A student on this product is a customer, and «чи» is not
suitable for one. Formal is not the same as stiff — the English has jokes and
energy, and that should survive. Do not flatten it into textbook prose.

Quotes take «...». Proper names take their standard Mongolian forms. Product
names and symbols stay Latin.

> Note if you have seen earlier material: grades 6 and 7 are written in «чи»,
> which is now the wrong register. Do not copy their tone.

## Writing maths

Only the rules that change what you type:

1. **Do not put Mongolian words inside a formula.** Write the formula in
   symbols and the words outside it. (If a word truly must sit inside one,
   mark it and Build will wrap it correctly.)
2. **Letters stay Latin** — `x`, `h`, point names, function names — even in
   Mongolian prose. Gloss one on first use if it helps.
3. **Bold is `**like this**`.** Single asterisks do not work and will appear
   on screen as asterisks.
4. **Units:** см, кг, мл read fine in a sentence.
