Each lesson, and what the student must end up able to do:

### 1. `transversals`

**Able to:** Recognize parallel lines and a transversal; name the eight angles it forms as corresponding, alternate interior, alternate exterior, or same-side interior pairs — by position alone.

**The idea that carries it:** A transversal cutting two lines makes 8 angles. Name each pair by position: corresponding, alternate interior/exterior, same-side interior.

**Where students break** — the English warns about these, and they are
worth keeping however you phrase them:
- Thinking any two lines with a transversal must be parallel.
- Confusing interior with exterior.

### 2. `angles-and-parallel-lines`

**Able to:** Apply the parallel-line angle relationships: corresponding, alternate interior, and alternate exterior angles are congruent; same-side interior angles are supplementary.

**The idea that carries it:** Parallel lines cut by a transversal: corresponding, alternate interior, alternate exterior → CONGRUENT. Same-side interior → SUPPLEMENTARY (180°).

**Where students break** — the English warns about these, and they are
worth keeping however you phrase them:
- Calling same-side interior angles congruent.
- Using these relationships when the lines aren't parallel.

**2 worked examples.** Yours to choose — different numbers, a
different context, a Mongolian setting instead of an American one. What
each must *do* is fixed:
- `pa-we1` — Parallel lines are cut by a transversal; one angle is $118^\circ$. Its corresponding angle measures —
- `pa-we2` — Same parallel lines: an interior angle is $118^\circ$. Its same-side interior partner measures —

**2 try-it problems**, same freedom and same condition.

### 3. `finding-angle-measures`

**Able to:** Find all eight angles formed by a transversal and parallel lines from one known angle, and solve for a variable using the relationships.

**The idea that carries it:** One angle fixes all eight: each is the angle or its supplement. For algebra, set equal pairs equal and supplementary pairs to 180, then solve.

**Where students break** — the English warns about these, and they are
worth keeping however you phrase them:
- Setting supplementary angles equal (or vice versa).
- Stopping after finding $x$ when asked for the angle.

**2 worked examples.** Yours to choose — different numbers, a
different context, a Mongolian setting instead of an American one. What
each must *do* is fixed:
- `fm-we1` — Parallel lines cut by a transversal; $\angle 1 = 112^\circ$. Find $\angle 4$ (its vertical angle) and $\angle 2$ (its linear pair).
- `fm-we2` — Corresponding angles are $(3x)^\circ$ and $(2x + 40)^\circ$. Solve for $x$.

**2 try-it problems**, same freedom and same condition.

### 4. `proving-lines-parallel`

**Able to:** Use the converses of the parallel-line theorems to prove two lines are parallel from an angle relationship.

**The idea that carries it:** Angle facts reverse: congruent corresponding / alternate angles (or supplementary same-side interior) PROVE the lines are parallel.

**Where students break** — the English warns about these, and they are
worth keeping however you phrase them:
- Requiring same-side interior angles to be equal to prove parallel.
- Concluding parallel from angles that aren't a valid pair.

**2 worked examples.** Yours to choose — different numbers, a
different context, a Mongolian setting instead of an American one. What
each must *do* is fixed:
- `pp-we1` — A transversal makes corresponding angles of $70^\circ$ and $70^\circ$. Are the two lines parallel?
- `pp-we2` — Same-side interior angles measure $105^\circ$ and $80^\circ$. Parallel?

**2 try-it problems**, same freedom and same condition.

### 5. `perpendicular-lines`

**Able to:** Define perpendicular lines, use the four right angles they create, and apply the fact that two lines perpendicular to the same line are parallel.

**The idea that carries it:** Perpendicular = meets at 90°, giving four right angles. Two lines perpendicular to the same line are parallel.

**Where students break** — the English warns about these, and they are
worth keeping however you phrase them:
- Thinking perpendicular lines make only one right angle.
- Calling the split pieces of a right angle supplementary.

**2 worked examples.** Yours to choose — different numbers, a
different context, a Mongolian setting instead of an American one. What
each must *do* is fixed:
- `pe-we1` — Line $m \perp$ line $n$. One of the four angles is $90^\circ$. What are the other three?
- `pe-we2` — A ray splits a right angle into $(2x)^\circ$ and $(x)^\circ$. Find $x$.

**2 try-it problems**, same freedom and same condition.

### 6. `proofs-with-parallel-lines`

**Able to:** Write two-column proofs that use the parallel-line relationships, and prove the Alternate Interior Angles Theorem from the Corresponding Angles Postulate.

**The idea that carries it:** Chain Unit 3's angle rules (and their converses) as reasons. The alternate interior theorem follows from corresponding + vertical + Transitive.

**Where students break** — the English warns about these, and they are
worth keeping however you phrase them:
- Using a theorem to prove itself.
- Skipping the reason on an angle step.

**2 worked examples.** Yours to choose — different numbers, a
different context, a Mongolian setting instead of an American one. What
each must *do* is fixed:
- `pf-we1` — Parallel lines cut by a transversal; $\angle 1 = 118^\circ$ and $\angle 1, \angle 5$ are corresponding. Prove $\angle 5 = 118^\circ$.
- `pf-we2` — $\angle 3$ and $\angle 5$ are same-side interior on parallel lines, with $m\angle 3 = (2x)^\circ$ and $m\angle 5 = (x + 30)^\circ$. Prove $x = 50$.

**2 try-it problems**, same freedom and same condition.

## What you owe for every example you change

A worked example or try-it problem with different numbers needs a new
**check** — a line of maths that says what must be true, so we can verify it
mechanically without a second person re-solving it.

**You do not write these.** Write the example and its answer in plain
Mongolian, and Build turns it into the assertion. If an example's answer
cannot be stated exactly, the example is not ready.

**Practice and test-yourself problems are different: do not change their
numbers.** Those are graded against a stored answer key and every student's
history is keyed to them. Rewrite the *wording* freely; leave the maths.

### Careful — English uses one word where Mongolian uses two

These are the ones that go wrong silently. The word above is the sense
this topic most likely means; check it against what you are actually
writing, and say so if the topic needs the other one.

**measure** — proposed **хэмжих**

> POLYSEMOUS — Mongolian splits what English keeps as one word, so name the sense. (1) The verb "to measure" = хэмжих (this entry). (2) "a measure of centre/spread" = хэмжүүр: shipped «аль төвийн хэмжүүр нөхцөл байдалд тохирохыг сонгоно», «тархалтын хамгийн энгийн хэмжүүр». (3) "a measurement" (one reading taken) = хэмжилт: «ганц хэмжилт, олон янз байдал алга». (4) A measurable quantity = хэмжигдэхүүн, which is the ministry's title for the whole measurement strand (MoE 10.12 «Хэмжигдэхүүн») and also the angle-measure noun хэмжээ (MoE 11.6 «Өнцгийн радиан хэмжээ»). Using хэмжих where хэмжүүр is meant is the likely error in statistics lessons.

## How it should sound

**«та», formal.** A student on this product is a customer, and «чи» is not
suitable for one. Formal is not the same as stiff — the English has jokes and
energy, and that should survive. Do not flatten it into textbook prose.

Quotes take «...». Proper names take their standard Mongolian forms. Product
names and symbols stay Latin.

> Note if you have seen earlier material: grades 6 and 7 are written in «чи»,
> which is now the wrong register. Do not copy their tone.

## Writing maths

Only the rules that change what you type:

1. **Do not put Mongolian words inside a formula.** Write the formula in
   symbols and the words outside it. (If a word truly must sit inside one,
   mark it and Build will wrap it correctly.)
2. **Letters stay Latin** — `x`, `h`, point names, function names — even in
   Mongolian prose. Gloss one on first use if it helps.
3. **Bold is `**like this**`.** Single asterisks do not work and will appear
   on screen as asterisks.
4. **Units:** см, кг, мл read fine in a sentence.
