## What this topic must teach

A student finishes this topic holding these ideas. Everything else in the
brief serves them.

1. **A parabola is a shape you can read.** Vertex, axis of symmetry, intercepts and direction of opening are all visible in the algebra before anything is plotted. The topic is about learning to read them off, not about plotting points and joining them up.

2. **Vertex form and standard form say the same thing differently.** Each one hands you a different fact for free — vertex form the turning point, standard form the y-intercept. Converting between them is worth doing precisely because it is the same function.

3. **Maximum and minimum are the same idea as the vertex.** Real questions about profit, height and area are vertex questions wearing a context. If a student sees that, word problems stop being a separate skill.

**The error to design against:** Sign of the vertex's x-coordinate — the formula gives −b/2a, and the minus is dropped constantly.

If your Mongolian version lands those and a student can do the practice
set, the topic is right — however you got there.

Each lesson, and what the student must end up able to do:

### 1. `meet-the-parabola`

**Able to:** Graph $y = ax^2$ from a table, name the vertex and axis of symmetry, and predict how $a$ flips, widens, or narrows the curve.

**The idea that carries it:** y = ax² is a symmetric U with vertex (0,0): a > 0 opens up, a < 0 down; bigger |a| = narrower.

**Where students break** — the English warns about these, and they are
worth keeping however you phrase them:
- Thinking $y = -x^2$ means "negative $x$, squared".
- Reading "bigger $a$" as "bigger, wider curve".

**3 worked examples.** Yours to choose — different numbers, a
different context, a Mongolian setting instead of an American one. What
each must *do* is fixed:
- `qf1-we1` — Table $y = x^2$ at $x = -2, -1, 0, 1, 2$ and name the vertex.
- `qf1-we2` — Does $y = -2x^2$ open up or down, and is it wider or narrower than $y = x^2$?
- `qf1-we3` — Verify the symmetry of $y = x^2$ at $x = 3$ and $x = -3$.

**2 try-it problems**, same freedom and same condition.

### 2. `vertex-form`

**Able to:** Read the vertex $(h, k)$ and axis $x = h$ straight from $y = a(x-h)^2 + k$, and translate between the shift and the equation.

**The idea that carries it:** y = a(x−h)² + k: vertex (h, k), axis x = h. The bracket's zero is h — sign flipped from what it looks like.

**Where students break** — the English warns about these, and they are
worth keeping however you phrase them:
- Reading $(x+3)^2$ as "right 3".
- Swapping h and k.

**3 worked examples.** Yours to choose — different numbers, a
different context, a Mongolian setting instead of an American one. What
each must *do* is fixed:
- `qf2-we1` — Vertex and axis of $y = (x-3)^2 + 2$?
- `qf2-we2` — Vertex of $y = -2(x+1)^2 + 8$, and is it a max or min?
- `qf2-we3` — Write the equation of $y = x^2$ shifted right 4 and down 1, and give its vertex.

**2 try-it problems**, same freedom and same condition.

### 3. `standard-form-features`

**Able to:** From $y = ax^2 + bx + c$, read the y-intercept $(0, c)$, compute the axis $x = -\frac{b}{2a}$, and find the vertex by substituting.

**The idea that carries it:** In y = ax² + bx + c: y-intercept (0, c); axis x = −b/2a; vertex by substituting that x back in.

**Where students break** — the English warns about these, and they are
worth keeping however you phrase them:
- Dropping the minus in $x = -\tfrac{b}{2a}$.
- Calling $(0, c)$ the vertex.

**3 worked examples.** Yours to choose — different numbers, a
different context, a Mongolian setting instead of an American one. What
each must *do* is fixed:
- `qf3-we1` — Axis, vertex, and y-intercept of $y = x^2 - 4x + 3$?
- `qf3-we2` — Vertex of $y = -x^2 + 6x - 5$, and is it a max or min?
- `qf3-we3` — Find the vertex of $y = 2x^2 + 8x + 3$.

**2 try-it problems**, same freedom and same condition.

### 4. `roots-and-x-intercepts`

**Able to:** Connect x-intercepts to the solutions of $ax^2+bx+c=0$, read roots from factored form, and predict the crossing count with the discriminant.

**The idea that carries it:** x-intercepts = roots of the equation. Factored form shows them; the discriminant counts them; the vertex explains them.

**Where students break** — the English warns about these, and they are
worth keeping however you phrase them:
- Reading the crossings of $y=(x-1)(x-3)$ as $-1$ and $-3$.
- Thinking "no x-intercepts" means "no graph".

**3 worked examples.** Yours to choose — different numbers, a
different context, a Mongolian setting instead of an American one. What
each must *do* is fixed:
- `qf4-we1` — Find the x-intercepts of $y = x^2 - 4x + 3$ and check them on the axis of symmetry.
- `qf4-we2` — Where does $y = (x+2)(x-5)$ cross the x-axis, and where is its axis of symmetry?
- `qf4-we3` — How many x-intercepts does $y = x^2 + 2x + 5$ have?

**2 try-it problems**, same freedom and same condition.

### 5. `max-min-problems`

**Able to:** Use the vertex to answer optimization questions: maximum height, maximum product, and maximum area with a fixed perimeter.

**The idea that carries it:** Optimization = vertex-finding: x = −b/2a locates the best input, substituting gives the best value.

**Where students break** — the English warns about these, and they are
worth keeping however you phrase them:
- Answering the vertex's x when the question asks the max VALUE (or vice versa).
- Maximizing area with a lopsided rectangle "because longer is bigger".

**3 worked examples.** Yours to choose — different numbers, a
different context, a Mongolian setting instead of an American one. What
each must *do* is fixed:
- `qf5-we1` — A ball follows $h = 20t - 5t^2$. Find the peak height and when it lands.
- `qf5-we2` — Two numbers add to $16$. How large can their product be?
- `qf5-we3` — With $24$ m of fencing, what's the biggest rectangular area?

**2 try-it problems**, same freedom and same condition.

### 6. `graphing-quadratics`

**Able to:** Graph any quadratic from standard form with the five-step recipe, using symmetry to double every plotted point for free.

**The idea that carries it:** Direction → y-intercept → axis → vertex → roots; then mirror every point across the axis for free.

**Where students break** — the English warns about these, and they are
worth keeping however you phrase them:
- Plotting the vertex and y-intercept, then freehanding a lopsided curve.
- Skipping the direction check and drawing every parabola opening up.

**3 worked examples.** Yours to choose — different numbers, a
different context, a Mongolian setting instead of an American one. What
each must *do* is fixed:
- `qf6-we1` — Run the full recipe on $y = x^2 - 2x - 3$.
- `qf6-we2` — Recipe for $y = -x^2 + 4x$ (no constant term).
- `qf6-we3` — Sketch data for $y = 2x^2 + 4x - 6$.

**2 try-it problems**, same freedom and same condition.

## What you owe for every example you change

A worked example or try-it problem with different numbers needs a new
**check** — a line of maths that says what must be true, so we can verify it
mechanically without a second person re-solving it.

**You do not write these.** Write the example and its answer in plain
Mongolian, and Build turns it into the assertion. If an example's answer
cannot be stated exactly, the example is not ready.

**Practice and test-yourself problems are different: do not change their
numbers.** Those are graded against a stored answer key and every student's
history is keyed to them. Rewrite the *wording* freely; leave the maths.

### Careful — English uses one word where Mongolian uses two

These are the ones that go wrong silently. The word above is the sense
this topic most likely means; check it against what you are actually
writing, and say so if the topic needs the other one.

**count** — proposed **тоолох**

> POLYSEMOUS. Verb "to count" = тоолох (ministry 12.14, and shipped throughout). Noun "the count" = тоо when it means how many items («Mean = sum ÷ count» → «Дундаж = нийлбэр ÷ тоо»), but тооллого when it means the tally/inventory itself («the small raised exponent is the count» → «илтгэгч нь тооллого»; "take inventory" → «Тооллого хий»). ## How it should sound

**«та», formal.** A student on this product is a customer, and «чи» is not
suitable for one. Formal is not the same as stiff — the English has jokes and
energy, and that should survive. Do not flatten it into textbook prose.

Quotes take «...». Proper names take their standard Mongolian forms. Product
names and symbols stay Latin.

> Note if you have seen earlier material: grades 6 and 7 are written in «чи»,
> which is now the wrong register. Do not copy their tone.

## Writing maths

Only the rules that change what you type:

1. **Do not put Mongolian words inside a formula.** Write the formula in
   symbols and the words outside it. (If a word truly must sit inside one,
   mark it and Build will wrap it correctly.)
2. **Letters stay Latin** — `x`, `h`, point names, function names — even in
   Mongolian prose. Gloss one on first use if it helps.
3. **Bold is `**like this**`.** Single asterisks do not work and will appear
   on screen as asterisks.
4. **Units:** см, кг, мл read fine in a sentence.
