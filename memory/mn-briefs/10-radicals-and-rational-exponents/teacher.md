## What this topic must teach

A student finishes this topic holding these ideas. Everything else in the
brief serves them.

1. **A radical IS a fractional exponent.** Not a related notation — the same object written twice. Once a student believes this, every exponent law they already know transfers to radicals for free, and the topic more than halves in size.

2. **Simplifying means pulling out perfect powers.** There is one move, applied repeatedly. The rest is arithmetic.

3. **√(x²) = |x|, not x.** This is the topic's honest difficulty and it is worth the time. A square root returns the non-negative root, so the absolute value is not pedantry — it is what keeps the algebra true for negative x.

**The error to design against:** Distributing a root over a sum: √(a+b) is not √a + √b. Reliable, and worth an explicit counterexample with numbers.

If your Mongolian version lands those and a student can do the practice
set, the topic is right — however you got there.

Each lesson, and what the student must end up able to do:

### 1. `simplifying-radicals`

**Able to:** Simplify square roots (and cube roots) by factoring out perfect squares (and cubes), using $\sqrt{ab} = \sqrt{a}\sqrt{b}$.

**The idea that carries it:** √(ab) = √a·√b: pull the largest perfect square out front. Products split; sums never do.

**Where students break** — the English warns about these, and they are
worth keeping however you phrase them:
- Splitting a SUM: $\sqrt{9+16} = 3 + 4 = 7$.
- Stopping at a small square factor: $\sqrt{72} = 3\sqrt{8}$ and calling it done.

**3 worked examples.** Yours to choose — different numbers, a
different context, a Mongolian setting instead of an American one. What
each must *do* is fixed:
- `rd1-we1` — Simplify $\sqrt{50}$.
- `rd1-we2` — Simplify $\sqrt{72}$.
- `rd1-we3` — Simplify $\sqrt[3]{54}$.

**2 try-it problems**, same freedom and same condition.

### 2. `operations-with-radicals`

**Able to:** Add and subtract like radicals (simplifying first when needed), and multiply radicals including distributed products.

**The idea that carries it:** Same root = like terms: combine the counts. Multiply anything: numbers×numbers, roots×roots.

**Where students break** — the English warns about these, and they are
worth keeping however you phrase them:
- Adding unlike radicals: $\sqrt{2} + \sqrt{3} = \sqrt{5}$.
- Multiplying the counts into the root: $(3\sqrt{2})(4\sqrt{5}) = \sqrt{120}$.

**3 worked examples.** Yours to choose — different numbers, a
different context, a Mongolian setting instead of an American one. What
each must *do* is fixed:
- `rd2-we1` — Add $3\sqrt{2} + 5\sqrt{2}$.
- `rd2-we2` — Simplify $\sqrt{8} + \sqrt{18}$.
- `rd2-we3` — Expand $(1+\sqrt{2})(3-\sqrt{2})$.

**2 try-it problems**, same freedom and same condition.

### 3. `rationalizing-denominators`

**Able to:** Rationalize denominators — single radicals by multiplying by themselves, binomials like $\sqrt{5}-2$ by their conjugates.

**The idea that carries it:** Multiply top and bottom by √n (single) or the conjugate (binomial) — ×1 in disguise; the bottom goes rational.

**Where students break** — the English warns about these, and they are
worth keeping however you phrase them:
- Multiplying only the denominator by $\sqrt{3}$.
- Using the same sign instead of the conjugate: multiplying $\sqrt5-2$ by $\sqrt5-2$.

**3 worked examples.** Yours to choose — different numbers, a
different context, a Mongolian setting instead of an American one. What
each must *do* is fixed:
- `rd3-we1` — Rationalize $\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}$.
- `rd3-we2` — Rationalize and simplify $\frac{6}{\sqrt{2}}$.
- `rd3-we3` — Rationalize $\frac{3}{\sqrt{5}-2}$.

**2 try-it problems**, same freedom and same condition.

### 4. `rational-exponents`

**Able to:** Convert between radical and exponent notation ($x^{m/n} = \sqrt[n]{x^m}$) and compute with rational exponents using the exponent laws.

**The idea that carries it:** x^(m/n) = (ⁿ√x)^m — bottom is the root, top is the power. Every exponent law still applies.

**Where students break** — the English warns about these, and they are
worth keeping however you phrase them:
- Reading $x^{m/n}$ upside down — root from the top, power from the bottom.
- Treating $x^{1/2}$ as $\tfrac{x}{2}$.

**3 worked examples.** Yours to choose — different numbers, a
different context, a Mongolian setting instead of an American one. What
each must *do* is fixed:
- `rd4-we1` — Evaluate $9^{1/2}$ and $8^{1/3}$.
- `rd4-we2` — Evaluate $8^{2/3}$.
- `rd4-we3` — Evaluate $16^{-1/2}$ and $27^{2/3}$.

**2 try-it problems**, same freedom and same condition.

### 5. `solving-radical-equations`

**Able to:** Solve radical equations by isolating the radical and squaring both sides, always checking for extraneous solutions.

**The idea that carries it:** Isolate the radical, square both sides, solve — then check every answer in the ORIGINAL equation.

**Where students break** — the English warns about these, and they are
worth keeping however you phrase them:
- Squaring before isolating: $(\sqrt{2x} + 3)^2 = 2x + 9$.
- Skipping the check because the algebra 'looks right'.

**3 worked examples.** Yours to choose — different numbers, a
different context, a Mongolian setting instead of an American one. What
each must *do* is fixed:
- `rd5-we1` — Solve $\sqrt{x+3} = 5$.
- `rd5-we2` — Solve $\sqrt{2x} + 3 = 7$.
- `rd5-we3` — Solve $\sqrt{x} = x - 2$.

**2 try-it problems**, same freedom and same condition.

### 6. `radical-formulas-in-the-wild`

**Able to:** Evaluate radical formulas from science, and invert them (solve radical equations) to answer backwards questions.

**The idea that carries it:** Radical formulas run forwards by evaluating and backwards by squaring — and under a root, doubling the output costs 4× the input.

**Where students break** — the English warns about these, and they are
worth keeping however you phrase them:
- Expecting doubled input to double the output.
- Forgetting to square when driving a formula backwards.

**3 worked examples.** Yours to choose — different numbers, a
different context, a Mongolian setting instead of an American one. What
each must *do* is fixed:
- `rd6-we1` — Skid marks: $v = \sqrt{20d}$. Find $v$ when $d = 45$ m.
- `rd6-we2` — Free fall: $t = \sqrt{h/5}$. How long to fall $80$ m?
- `rd6-we3` — Backwards: with $v = \sqrt{20d}$, what skid length gives $v = 40$ m/s?

**2 try-it problems**, same freedom and same condition.

## What you owe for every example you change

A worked example or try-it problem with different numbers needs a new
**check** — a line of maths that says what must be true, so we can verify it
mechanically without a second person re-solving it.

**You do not write these.** Write the example and its answer in plain
Mongolian, and Build turns it into the assertion. If an example's answer
cannot be stated exactly, the example is not ready.

**Practice and test-yourself problems are different: do not change their
numbers.** Those are graded against a stored answer key and every student's
history is keyed to them. Rewrite the *wording* freely; leave the maths.

### Careful — English uses one word where Mongolian uses two

These are the ones that go wrong silently. The word above is the sense
this topic most likely means; check it against what you are actually
writing, and say so if the topic needs the other one.

**factor** — proposed **хуваагч**

> One English key, two live Mongolian terms: a number's factor/divisor is «хуваагч» (164× in production, not in the ministry standard), while an algebraic factor is «үржигдэхүүн» (ministry 10.2 «үржигдэхүүн болгон задлах»; 80× in production). Needs two keys — and note «хуваагдагч» (105×) is the multiple/dividend, easy to confuse.

**count** — proposed **тоолох**

> POLYSEMOUS. Verb "to count" = тоолох (ministry 12.14, and shipped throughout). Noun "the count" = тоо when it means how many items («Mean = sum ÷ count» → «Дундаж = нийлбэр ÷ тоо»), but тооллого when it means the tally/inventory itself («the small raised exponent is the count» → «илтгэгч нь тооллого»; "take inventory" → «Тооллого хий»). This entry proposes the verb. Do not write тоолол.

## How it should sound

**«та», formal.** A student on this product is a customer, and «чи» is not
suitable for one. Formal is not the same as stiff — the English has jokes and
energy, and that should survive. Do not flatten it into textbook prose.

Quotes take «...». Proper names take their standard Mongolian forms. Product
names and symbols stay Latin.

> Note if you have seen earlier material: grades 6 and 7 are written in «чи»,
> which is now the wrong register. Do not copy their tone.

## Writing maths

Only the rules that change what you type:

1. **Do not put Mongolian words inside a formula.** Write the formula in
   symbols and the words outside it. (If a word truly must sit inside one,
   mark it and Build will wrap it correctly.)
2. **Letters stay Latin** — `x`, `h`, point names, function names — even in
   Mongolian prose. Gloss one on first use if it helps.
3. **Bold is `**like this**`.** Single asterisks do not work and will appear
   on screen as asterisks.
4. **Units:** см, кг, мл read fine in a sentence.
