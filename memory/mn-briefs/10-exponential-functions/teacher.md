## What this topic must teach

A student finishes this topic holding these ideas. Everything else in the
brief serves them.

1. **Exponential growth is repeated multiplication, and that is why it wins.** The comparison against a straight line is the topic's whole point: a student needs to see that a slower-starting exponential overtakes any linear function, and to feel why.

2. **The base tells you everything.** Greater than 1 grows, between 0 and 1 decays, and the distance from 1 sets the speed. One number, read directly.

3. **Percent change and the base are the same fact.** A 5% rise per year IS a base of 1.05. Students who hold these as two separate ideas cannot model anything real.

**The error to design against:** Reading a 20% decrease as a base of 0.2 rather than 0.8.

If your Mongolian version lands those and a student can do the practice
set, the topic is right — however you got there.

Each lesson, and what the student must end up able to do:

### 1. `meet-exponential-functions`

**Able to:** Recognize and evaluate exponential functions $y = a \cdot b^x$, identifying the starting value $a$ and the growth factor $b$.

**The idea that carries it:** y = a·bˣ: start at a, multiply by b each step. Constant ratio = exponential; constant difference = linear.

**Where students break** — the English warns about these, and they are
worth keeping however you phrase them:
- Computing $a \cdot b^x$ as $(a \cdot b)^x$.
- Calling $y = x^2$ exponential because it 'has an exponent'.

**3 worked examples.** Yours to choose — different numbers, a
different context, a Mongolian setting instead of an American one. What
each must *do* is fixed:
- `ef1-we1` — Evaluate $y = 3 \cdot 2^x$ at $x = 0, 1, 4$.
- `ef1-we2` — A colony starts at 5 and triples hourly: write the function and find the count after 3 hours.
- `ef1-we3` — Is the table $x: 0,1,2,3$ / $y: 2, 6, 18, 54$ linear or exponential?

**2 try-it problems**, same freedom and same condition.

### 2. `growth-and-decay`

**Able to:** Distinguish growth ($b > 1$) from decay ($0 < b < 1$) and convert percent change to a growth factor: $+p\%$ → $1 + \tfrac{p}{100}$, $-p\%$ → $1 - \tfrac{p}{100}$.

**The idea that carries it:** Factor above 1 grows, below 1 decays. From percent: b = 1 ± p/100 — the fraction you KEEP.

**Where students break** — the English warns about these, and they are
worth keeping however you phrase them:
- Using the percent itself as the factor: '$30\%$ decay → $b = 0.3$'.
- Treating decay as subtraction: '$-25\%$ yearly = $-200$ every year from 800'.

**3 worked examples.** Yours to choose — different numbers, a
different context, a Mongolian setting instead of an American one. What
each must *do* is fixed:
- `ef2-we1` — Write the factor: (a) grows $20\%$/yr, (b) loses $40\%$/yr.
- `ef2-we2` — A \$800 phone loses $25\%$ of its value yearly. Value after 2 years?
- `ef2-we3` — A town of $2000$ grows $10\%$ a year. Population after 3 years?

**2 try-it problems**, same freedom and same condition.

### 3. `exponential-vs-linear`

**Able to:** Compare exponential and linear growth, find where the exponential overtakes, and recognize why the lead is permanent.

**The idea that carries it:** Lines add, exponentials multiply. Every exponential (b > 1) overtakes every line — and never gives the lead back.

**Where students break** — the English warns about these, and they are
worth keeping however you phrase them:
- Concluding from early data that the line 'grows faster'.
- Thinking a steeper line can hold off an exponential.

**3 worked examples.** Yours to choose — different numbers, a
different context, a Mongolian setting instead of an American one. What
each must *do* is fixed:
- `ef3-we1` — Offer A pays $8$/day; offer B doubles from $1$: $2^x$ on day $x$. Compare daily pay on days 3 and 6.
- `ef3-we2` — Total coins by end of day 5: A ($8$/day) vs B (doubling $1, 2, 4, \ldots$)?
- `ef3-we3` — When does $2^x$ first EXCEED $8x$ (whole days)?

**2 try-it problems**, same freedom and same condition.

### 4. `compound-interest`

**Able to:** Use $A = P(1+r)^t$ to compute compound growth, and contrast it with simple (non-compounding) interest.

**The idea that carries it:** A = P(1+r)^t — the balance multiplies by (1+r) each period, so interest earns interest.

**Where students break** — the English warns about these, and they are
worth keeping however you phrase them:
- Computing compound interest as rate × years: '$10\%$ for 3 years = $+30\%$'.
- Using the percent as $r$: $A = P(1+10)^t$ for $10\%$.

**3 worked examples.** Yours to choose — different numbers, a
different context, a Mongolian setting instead of an American one. What
each must *do* is fixed:
- `ef4-we1` — $100$ at $10\%$ compounded yearly: balance after 2 years?
- `ef4-we2` — $500$ at $20\%$/year for 3 years, compounded. Final amount?
- `ef4-we3` — $1000$ for 2 years: compound $10\%$ vs simple $10\%$ — the difference?

**2 try-it problems**, same freedom and same condition.

### 5. `half-life-and-decay`

**Able to:** Model half-life and other decay processes with $y = a \cdot (\tfrac{1}{2})^{t/h}$ and factor-based decay models.

**The idea that carries it:** Half-life: y = a·(1/2)^(t/h) — the exponent counts halvings. Other decay: power the keep-factor.

**Where students break** — the English warns about these, and they are
worth keeping however you phrase them:
- Using the TIME as the exponent instead of the number of halvings.
- Thinking two half-lives = all gone.

**3 worked examples.** Yours to choose — different numbers, a
different context, a Mongolian setting instead of an American one. What
each must *do* is fixed:
- `ef5-we1` — 400 mg of medicine, half-life 6 h. Amount at $t = 18$ h?
- `ef5-we2` — A 96-g radioactive sample has half-life 5 days. Amount after 20 days?
- `ef5-we3` — A car worth 20,000 loses $15\%$ yearly. Value after 2 years?

**2 try-it problems**, same freedom and same condition.

### 6. `building-exponential-models`

**Able to:** Write $y = a \cdot b^x$ from stories and tables (finding $b$ as a ratio), and use the model to forecast.

**The idea that carries it:** a = the start, b = the consecutive ratio (or the gap-root). Write y = a·bˣ, then forecast — with a sanity check.

**Where students break** — the English warns about these, and they are
worth keeping however you phrase them:
- Taking $b$ as the DIFFERENCE between entries.
- Forecasting past the model's sanity range.

**3 worked examples.** Yours to choose — different numbers, a
different context, a Mongolian setting instead of an American one. What
each must *do* is fixed:
- `ef6-we1` — Cells: day 0 — 150, day 1 — 450, day 2 — 1350. Model and day-6 forecast?
- `ef6-we2` — A quantity drops $64 \to 4$ in 2 steps. Find $b$ and the model.
- `ef6-we3` — "A rumor starts with 5 people and each person tells it so it triples hourly." Model and hour-4 count?

**2 try-it problems**, same freedom and same condition.

## What you owe for every example you change

A worked example or try-it problem with different numbers needs a new
**check** — a line of maths that says what must be true, so we can verify it
mechanically without a second person re-solving it.

**You do not write these.** Write the example and its answer in plain
Mongolian, and Build turns it into the assertion. If an example's answer
cannot be stated exactly, the example is not ready.

**Practice and test-yourself problems are different: do not change their
numbers.** Those are graded against a stored answer key and every student's
history is keyed to them. Rewrite the *wording* freely; leave the maths.

### Careful — English uses one word where Mongolian uses two

These are the ones that go wrong silently. The word above is the sense
this topic most likely means; check it against what you are actually
writing, and say so if the topic needs the other one.

**factor** — proposed **хуваагч**

> One English key, two live Mongolian terms: a number's factor/divisor is «хуваагч» (164× in production, not in the ministry standard), while an algebraic factor is «үржигдэхүүн» (ministry 10.2 «үржигдэхүүн болгон задлах»; 80× in production). Needs two keys — and note «хуваагдагч» (105×) is the multiple/dividend, easy to confuse.

**count** — proposed **тоолох**

> POLYSEMOUS. Verb "to count" = тоолох (ministry 12.14, and shipped throughout). Noun "the count" = тоо when it means how many items («Mean = sum ÷ count» → «Дундаж = нийлбэр ÷ тоо»), but тооллого when it means the tally/inventory itself («the small raised exponent is the count» → «илтгэгч нь тооллого»; "take inventory" → «Тооллого хий»). This entry proposes the verb. Do not write тоолол.

**half** — proposed **хагас**

> POLYSEMOUS. хагас is the noun "a half" (the quantity 1/2) and is what this entry proposes. But when "half" means one of two parts of a set or a figure, shipped Mongolian uses тал: "half at or below, half at or above" → «тал нь түүнээс дээшгүй, тал нь доошгүй»; "each exactly half the slice" → «тус бүр нь талхны яг тал»; "The triangle is exactly half of it" → «Гурвалжин чинь яг тал нь». Rule of thumb: хагас for the computed amount, тал for one of the two portions. тал is also "side" (of a shape, of an equation), so it carries real ambiguity — prefer хагас wherever the amount is meant.

## How it should sound

**«та», formal.** A student on this product is a customer, and «чи» is not
suitable for one. Formal is not the same as stiff — the English has jokes and
energy, and that should survive. Do not flatten it into textbook prose.

Quotes take «...». Proper names take their standard Mongolian forms. Product
names and symbols stay Latin.

> Note if you have seen earlier material: grades 6 and 7 are written in «чи»,
> which is now the wrong register. Do not copy their tone.

## Writing maths

Only the rules that change what you type:

1. **Do not put Mongolian words inside a formula.** Write the formula in
   symbols and the words outside it. (If a word truly must sit inside one,
   mark it and Build will wrap it correctly.)
2. **Letters stay Latin** — `x`, `h`, point names, function names — even in
   Mongolian prose. Gloss one on first use if it helps.
3. **Bold is `**like this**`.** Single asterisks do not work and will appear
   on screen as asterisks.
4. **Units:** см, кг, мл read fine in a sentence.
