## What this topic must teach

A student finishes this topic holding these ideas. Everything else in the
brief serves them.

1. **All four are slices of one cone.** Circle, ellipse, parabola, hyperbola differ only by the angle of the cut. Teaching them as four unrelated equations to memorise is what makes this topic feel enormous when it is small.

2. **Completing the square is how you read a conic.** The general second-degree equation says almost nothing; the standard form hands you the centre, the radii and the orientation directly. The whole skill is getting from one to the other.

3. **The signs between the squared terms identify the curve.** Same sign is an ellipse, opposite signs a hyperbola, one squared term a parabola. A student can classify before solving anything.

**The error to design against:** Forgetting to balance both sides when completing the square — adding a term inside a bracket that is multiplied by a coefficient, without multiplying it on the right.

If your Mongolian version lands those and a student can do the practice
set, the topic is right — however you got there.

Each lesson, and what the student must end up able to do:

### 1. `circles-as-equations`

**Able to:** Read and write circle equations (x−h)² + (y−k)² = r², extract center and radius, and recover standard form by completing the square.

**The idea that carries it:** (x−h)² + (y−k)² = r²: the distance formula squared. Signs flip for the center, the RHS is r², and completing the square recovers hidden centers.

**Where students break** — the English warns about these, and they are
worth keeping however you phrase them:
- Reading (x − 3)² as center x = −3.
- Reporting the radius as 25.

**3 worked examples.** Yours to choose — different numbers, a
different context, a Mongolian setting instead of an American one. What
each must *do* is fixed:
- `co1-we1` — Find the center and radius of $(x-3)^2 + (y+2)^2 = 25$.
- `co1-we2` — Write the equation of the circle centered $(-1, 4)$ with radius 6.
- `co1-we3` — Rewrite $x^2 + y^2 - 6x + 4y - 12 = 0$ in standard form.

**2 try-it problems**, same freedom and same condition.

### 2. `parabolas-focus-and-directrix`

**Able to:** Use the focus-directrix definition, connect x² = 4py to focal distance p, and find focus/directrix from equations and vice versa.

**The idea that carries it:** A parabola is the points equidistant from focus and directrix; x² = 4py puts the focus at (0, p). The reflection property makes it the shape of every dish and headlight.

**Where students break** — the English warns about these, and they are
worth keeping however you phrase them:
- Putting the focus at (0, 4p).
- Placing the directrix through the vertex.

**3 worked examples.** Yours to choose — different numbers, a
different context, a Mongolian setting instead of an American one. What
each must *do* is fixed:
- `co2-we1` — Find the focus and directrix of $x^2 = 8y$.
- `co2-we2` — Verify the definition at the point $(4, 2)$ on $x^2 = 8y$.
- `co2-we3` — A satellite dish is $y = \tfrac{x^2}{12}$ (meters). Where does the receiver go?

**2 try-it problems**, same freedom and same condition.

### 3. `ellipses`

**Able to:** Read a and b from the standard equation, find foci with c² = a² − b², compute eccentricity, and use the string (sum) property.

**The idea that carries it:** x²/a² + y²/b² = 1: distances to the two foci sum to 2a; the foci hide at c² = a² − b²; eccentricity c/a grades the stretch from circle (0) toward cigar (1).

**Where students break** — the English warns about these, and they are
worth keeping however you phrase them:
- Using c² = a² + b² for an ellipse.
- Reading a and b as the axis LENGTHS.

**3 worked examples.** Yours to choose — different numbers, a
different context, a Mongolian setting instead of an American one. What
each must *do* is fixed:
- `co3-we1` — For $\tfrac{x^2}{25} + \tfrac{y^2}{9} = 1$: find the axes, foci, and eccentricity.
- `co3-we2` — Verify the string property at $(0, 3)$ on that ellipse.
- `co3-we3` — Write the ellipse with foci $(\pm3, 0)$ and major axis 10.

**2 try-it problems**, same freedom and same condition.

### 4. `hyperbolas`

**Able to:** Read a, b from the standard hyperbola, draw asymptotes y = ±(b/a)x, find foci with c² = a² + b², and use the difference property.

**The idea that carries it:** x²/a² − y²/b² = 1: two branches on asymptotes y = ±(b/a)x, foci outside at c² = a² + b², and focal distances that DIFFER by 2a.

**Where students break** — the English warns about these, and they are
worth keeping however you phrase them:
- Using c² = a² − b² for a hyperbola.
- Expecting the branches to reach the asymptotes.

**3 worked examples.** Yours to choose — different numbers, a
different context, a Mongolian setting instead of an American one. What
each must *do* is fixed:
- `co4-we1` — For $\tfrac{x^2}{9} - \tfrac{y^2}{16} = 1$: vertices, asymptotes, foci.
- `co4-we2` — Verify the difference property at the vertex $(3, 0)$.
- `co4-we3` — Write the hyperbola with vertices $(\pm2, 0)$ and asymptotes $y = \pm3x$.

**2 try-it problems**, same freedom and same condition.

### 5. `identifying-conics`

**Able to:** Classify degree-2 equations into the four conic families by inspecting the squared terms, and convert to standard form to extract features.

**The idea that carries it:** Read the squared terms: equal → circle, same-sign → ellipse, opposite-sign → hyperbola, one missing → parabola. Complete squares to find where it moved.

**Where students break** — the English warns about these, and they are
worth keeping however you phrase them:
- Classifying by the constants instead of the squared terms.
- Completing the square without factoring the coefficient first.

**3 worked examples.** Yours to choose — different numbers, a
different context, a Mongolian setting instead of an American one. What
each must *do* is fixed:
- `co5-we1` — Classify and standardize: $4x^2 + 9y^2 = 36$.
- `co5-we2` — Classify: $x^2 - 4y^2 = 16$; $y^2 = 6x$; $x^2 + y^2 = 7$.
- `co5-we3` — Standardize the shifted conic $x^2 + 4y^2 - 2x + 16y + 13 = 0$.

**2 try-it problems**, same freedom and same condition.

### 6. `the-grand-tour`

**Able to:** Apply all four conics to mixed problems, choose the right curve and law for each situation, and close out the course.

**The idea that carries it:** Match the distance story to the curve, the focus position to the law — and notice the whole seven-year course was one connected build.

**Where students break** — the English warns about these, and they are
worth keeping however you phrase them:
- Answering the wrong extreme in orbit problems.
- Forgetting which law goes with which curve under exam pressure.

**3 worked examples.** Yours to choose — different numbers, a
different context, a Mongolian setting instead of an American one. What
each must *do* is fixed:
- `co6-we1` — A comet's orbit has $a = 20$ AU and eccentricity $e = 0.8$. How close does it come to the Sun (at a focus)?
- `co6-we2` — A lithotripsy machine is half an ellipse with $a = 25$ cm, $b = 15$ cm. The emitter sits at one focus; where must the kidney stone be?
- `co6-we3` — Classify each: a wheel's rim; a thrown ball's path; Halley's orbit; a sonic boom's ground curve.

**2 try-it problems**, same freedom and same condition.

## What you owe for every example you change

A worked example or try-it problem with different numbers needs a new
**check** — a line of maths that says what must be true, so we can verify it
mechanically without a second person re-solving it.

**You do not write these.** Write the example and its answer in plain
Mongolian, and Build turns it into the assertion. If an example's answer
cannot be stated exactly, the example is not ready.

**Practice and test-yourself problems are different: do not change their
numbers.** Those are graded against a stored answer key and every student's
history is keyed to them. Rewrite the *wording* freely; leave the maths.

### Careful — English uses one word where Mongolian uses two

These are the ones that go wrong silently. The word above is the sense
this topic most likely means; check it against what you are actually
writing, and say so if the topic needs the other one.

**shape** — proposed **дүрс**

> Production splits the word by sense: «дүрс» for a geometric shape (73×) and «хэлбэр» for the shape of a distribution ("Center, spread, shape" → «Төв, тархалт, хэлбэр»). «хэлбэр» is also this site's word for "form". Needs two keys — geometric shape vs. distribution shape.

**feature** — proposed **шинж**

> Split by sense in production: "шинж" where it means a property of the data, but "That sensitivity is a feature AND a warning" → "давуу тал" (advantage). As a term label "шинж" is the one to keep; the "давуу тал" line is prose, not a term, and needs no change.

**Section headings** are fixed too, because the shipped lessons already use
them and a student should meet the same words in every lesson:

**Бодсон жишээнүүд** · **Өөрөө туршиж үз** · **Түргэн шалгалт** · **Тоглож үз** · **Сонирхолтой баримт** · **Эргэн дүгнэлт** · **Юу сурснаа эргэн харъя**

## How it should sound

**«та», formal.** A student on this product is a customer, and «чи» is not
suitable for one. Formal is not the same as stiff — the English has jokes and
energy, and that should survive. Do not flatten it into textbook prose.

Quotes take «...». Proper names take their standard Mongolian forms. Product
names and symbols stay Latin.

> Note if you have seen earlier material: grades 6 and 7 are written in «чи»,
> which is now the wrong register. Do not copy their tone.

## Writing maths

Only the rules that change what you type:

1. **Do not put Mongolian words inside a formula.** Write the formula in
   symbols and the words outside it. (If a word truly must sit inside one,
   mark it and Build will wrap it correctly.)
2. **Letters stay Latin** — `x`, `h`, point names, function names — even in
   Mongolian prose. Gloss one on first use if it helps.
3. **Bold is `**like this**`.** Single asterisks do not work and will appear
   on screen as asterisks.
4. **Units:** см, кг, мл read fine in a sentence.
